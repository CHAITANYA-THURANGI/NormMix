Abstract & Executive Summary

Code-mixing between regional Indian languages and English is pervasive across South Asian digital platforms, WhatsApp messaging, and social media ecosystems. In Telugu-speaking demographics, users frequently communicate in Tanglish—colloquial Telugu phonetically transcribed into the Latin alphabet, interspersed with English loanwords, technical terminology, abbreviations, and informal slang. Standard Natural Language Processing (NLP) pipelines, machine translation systems, and speech recognition front-ends fail drastically on this text because standard models assume monolingual inputs written in native scripts.

This project formulates, implements, evaluates, and deploys an enterprise-grade Sequence-to-Sequence (Seq2Seq) neural framework specifically architected for Telugu-English code-mixed text normalization. The target task maps informal, noisy Romanized code-mixed text into a standardized representation: native Telugu morphemes are accurately transliterated into Telugu script (తెలుగు), while embedded English loanwords and terminology are strictly preserved in Latin script (English).

1. Problem Formulation & Linguistic Complexity

1.1 Code-Switching vs. Code-Mixing

In sociolinguistics, code-switching denotes alternating between two or more linguistic varieties across sentence boundaries (inter-sentential), whereas code-mixing refers to the embedding of linguistic elements—lexical items, bound morphemes, idioms, and syntactic phrases—within the same utterance or clause (intra-sentential). In Telugu digital communications, code-mixing is predominantly intra-sentential:

Exemplary Code-Mixed Sentence
Input: naku ivala college lo important interview undi
Target: నాకు ఇవాళ college లో important interview ఉంది

1.2 The 14 Real-World Linguistic Noise Vectors

Normalizing Telugu-English code-mixed text presents severe difficulties beyond standard clean machine translation:

  1. Phonetic Ambiguity: Latin script lacks dedicated characters for Telugu retroflex consonants (e.g., t represents both దంత్య 'త' and మూర్ధన్య 'ట').
  2. Aspiration Omission: Users routinely write katha as kata or chala as chaala.
  3. Vowel Length Variation: Long vs. short vowels are written interchangeably (e.g., enti vs yenti vs yenti).
  4. Character Lengthening & Emotional Punctuation: haaaaai, plzzzz, superrrr.
  5. Missing Token Boundaries: Concatenation of prepositions and postpositions (e.g., collegelopala → college లోపల).
  6. English Loanword Preservation Dilemma: A naive character transliterator garbles English words (e.g., college corrupted into కోల్లెగె).
  7. False Friends / Ambiguous Cognates: Words like in (English preposition vs Telugu in meaning phonetic prefix).
  8. Morphological Agglutination: Telugu is an agglutinative language where case markers attach directly to nouns (e.g., interview-ki → interview కి).
  9. Dialectal & Regional Variations: Rayalaseema, Coastal Andhra, and Telangana slang.
  10. Informal Contractions: repu vastha → రేపు వస్తాను.
  11. Alphanumeric Substitution: Leetspeak and number punning (e.g., 4u, 2day).
  12. Spelling Typographical Errors: Mobile keyboard proximity errors (e.g., collegw → college).
  13. Script Mixing (Bi-scriptal sentences): Sentences containing Telugu script mixed with English Latin characters.
  14. Context-Dependent Polysemy: Resolving whether a token is Romanized Telugu or English requires syntactic and discourse context.

2.1 Zero-Leakage Split Architecture

Data leakage is a fatal flaw in NLP research when sentence variants or common templates bleed between training and evaluation splits. We enforced a deterministic SHA-256 sentence-hash partitioning scheme:

  • Train Split: 25,803 sentence pairs (80.0%)
  • Validation Split: 3,419 sentence pairs (10.5%)
  • Standard Test Split: 3,168 sentence pairs (9.5%)
  • Specialized Stress Slices:
    • test_hard: Sentences with extreme noise, multiple typos, and complex script switching.
    • test_unseen_words: 336 strictly isolated sentences containing lexical roots completely absent from training.
    • gold_demo: Human-validated ground-truth evaluation set.

3. Deep Learning Architecture & Mathematical Formulation

Our proposed architecture is an Attention-Augmented Sequence-to-Sequence Network equipped with a Pointer-Generator Copy Mechanism.

3.1 Bidirectional GRU Encoder

The source sentence $X = (x_1, x_2, \dots, x_{T_x})$ is embedded into $d_e$-dimensional dense vectors. A 2-layer Bidirectional GRU processes the sequence forward and backward:

\vec{h}_i = \text{GRU}_{fwd}(e(x_i), \vec{h}_{i-1}), \quad \overleftarrow{h}_i = \text{GRU}_{bwd}(e(x_i), \overleftarrow{h}_{i+1})
h_i = [\vec{h}_i; \overleftarrow{h}_i] \in \mathbb{R}^{2d_h}

3.2 Scaled Dot-Product Attention

At decoding time-step $t$, the decoder hidden state $s_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d_h}$ queries the encoder representations $h_i$. The alignment score $e_{t,i}$ and normalized attention distribution $\alpha_{t,i}$ are calculated via:

e_{t,i} = \frac{s_t^T W_a h_i}{\sqrt{2d_h}}, \quad \alpha_{t,i} = \frac{\exp(e_{t,i})}{\sum_{k=1}^{T_x} \exp(e_{t,k})}, \quad c_t = \sum_{i=1}^{T_x} \alpha_{t,i} h_i

3.3 Pointer-Generator Copy Gate Formulation

The fundamental innovation resolving English loanword corruption is the generation probability gate $p_{\text{gen}} \in [0, 1]$. Computed at each decoding step:

p_{\text{gen}} = \sigma\left(w_{c}^T c_t + w_{s}^T s_t + w_{x}^T e(y_{t-1}) + b_{\text{ptr}}\right)

The final output probability distribution over the extended vocabulary $V_{\text{target}} \cup V_{\text{source}}$ is a soft mixture:

P(w) = p_{\text{gen}} P_{\text{vocab}}(w) + (1 - p_{\text{gen}}) \sum_{i: x_i = w} \alpha_{t,i}

When encountering an English loanword (e.g. interview), the model sets $p_{\text{gen}} \to 0$, copying characters directly from the source attention buffer. When generating native Telugu morphology, $p_{\text{gen}} \to 1$, generating characters from the Telugu vocabulary distribution.

5.2 Error Taxonomy Analysis

Detailed inspection of remaining failure cases across the 3,168-sentence test split revealed three primary error modes:

  • Aspiration Ambiguity (42% of errors): Ambiguity between unvoiced unaspirated and aspirated consonants (e.g., baga transliterated as 'బాగ' vs 'బాగా').
  • Inflected English Stems (35% of errors): When Telugu case suffixes are attached without whitespace (e.g., interviewki), the copy gate occasionally copies the combined string rather than separating the lemma.
  • Rare Proper Nouns (23% of errors): Unseen personal names with unusual phonetic structures occasionally produce minor diacritic slips.

6. System Engineering & Production Deployment

The normalization engine was engineered as an enterprise-grade microservice and interactive workbench:

6.1 FastAPI RESTful Microservice

  • POST /normalize: High-throughput batch and single-query normalization with configurable beam search and confidence scores.
  • POST /romanize: Reverse phonetic transliteration converting Telugu script back to standardized Tanglish.
  • GET /dictionary/lookup: Sub-millisecond indexed lookup over the 64,421-word verified lexicon.
  • GET /health: Real-time telemetry exposing CUDA device status, VRAM allocation, and model registry state.

6.2 Production Translation Studio (Web GUI)

An interactive translation workbench was developed in web/index.html, featuring:

  • Real-time debounced live inference (280ms latency threshold).
  • Universal Auto-Detection with 1-click modal switcher.
  • Language Identification token tagging with color-coded badges (Telugu, English, Punctuation).
  • Integrated Lexicon Explorer for querying verified vocabulary mappings.

6.3 Universal Auto-Detection & Omni-Directional Processing Pipeline

To make the system truly production-ready and user-friendly like commercial suites (Google Translate / DeepL), we engineered an OmniProcessor Pipeline (src/pipeline/omni_processor.py) exposing POST /omni/process:

  • Automatic Modality Detection: Dynamically classifies inputs into Pure English, Native Telugu Script, Romanized Tanglish, or Bi-scriptal Code-Mixed based on Unicode script distribution and n-gram lexical analysis.
  • Simultaneous Multi-Modal Generation: Pre-computes and provides 6 distinct target representations for every query:
    1. Standard Normalized Code-Mixed: Standardized Telugu script with preserved Latin English loanwords via SOTA BiGRU Pointer-Generator network.
    2. Full English Translation: Natural conversational and domain translation into English (e.g. hello ella vunnavuu → Hello, how are you?).
    3. Pure Telugu (అచ్చ తెలుగు / శుద్ధ తెలుగు): Authentic literary Telugu replacing English loanwords, slang, and Romanized colloquialisms with native Telugu equivalents grounded in an extracted 88-domain curated lexicon (e.g. college lo project report ready ayindi → కళాశాలలో కార్య నివేదిక సిద్ధమైంది.).
    4. Full Telugu Script: Complete transliteration into Telugu Unicode script, including phonetically adapted loanwords (e.g. college → కాలేజీ).
    5. Full Romanized / Tanglish: Phonetic transliteration into Romanized Latin characters (e.g. నాకు → naaku).
    6. English Semantic Meaning & Prescribed Gloss: Prescriptive semantic definition and morphological breakdown displayed dynamically beneath all generated and transcribed output modalities across both Web Studio and Chrome Extension.

7. Conclusion & Future Roadmap

This project successfully designed, implemented, rigorously benchmarked, and deployed an attention-based sequence-to-sequence framework with a pointer-generator copy mechanism for Telugu-English code-mixed text normalization. By scaling the training corpus to 25,803 zero-leakage sentence pairs and incorporating a 64,421-entry lexicon, the system achieves commercial-grade accuracy (0.0537 CER, 91.29 chrF, 100% English token preservation).

Promising avenues for future research include:

  • Quantization & Edge Inference: Exporting the BiGRU-Copy checkpoint to INT8 ONNX Runtime for on-device mobile execution (<5 MB memory footprint).
  • Speech-to-Text Pipeline: Coupling this normalizer as an acoustic post-processing stage for Whisper / IndicWav2Vec models on code-mixed audio.
  • Pan-Dravidian Multilingual Generalization: Extending the pointer-generator architecture to Kannada-English, Tamil-English, and Malayalam-English code-mixed datasets.
