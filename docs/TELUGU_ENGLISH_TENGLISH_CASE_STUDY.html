Evolution and Current Landscape of Telugu–English–Tenglish Translation

A Study of Translation, Transliteration, Code-Mixing, and Modern AI Systems
Academic Case Study & Research Monograph
Course Context: Advanced Artificial Intelligence & Neural Networks (AAN)
Published & Defended: October 2026 | Document Classification: Academic Research Review
ABSTRACT Natural Language Processing (NLP) for South Asian languages has undergone a profound paradigm shift over the past four decades, transitioning from symbolic rule-based expert systems to statistical modeling, sequence-to-sequence neural architectures, dense multilingual transformers, and contemporary generative Large Language Models (LLMs). While standardized machine translation between formal Telugu script (తెలుగు) and English is now considered a technologically mature discipline—demonstrated by state-of-the-art production systems such as Google Translate, Microsoft Translator, and AI4Bharat’s IndicTrans2—a critical technological gap persists in the processing of informal, digital vernacular communication. In real-world social media, instant messaging (WhatsApp, Telegram), and web platforms, native speakers predominantly communicate in Tenglish (Romanized Telugu) characterized by extensive intra-sentential code-mixing, unstandardized phonetic orthography, chat abbreviations, and English loanword insertions.

This monograph provides an academically rigorous investigation of the Telugu–English linguistic computing ecosystem. We trace the 40-year evolution across six technological epochs, evaluate the current commercial and academic landscape, formalize the Six-Directional Language Matrix, deconstruct why subword tokenizers fail on Romanized text, and examine the positioning of NormMix AI—an experimental prototype leveraging a 2-Layer BiGRU with Scaled Dot-Product Attention and a Pointer-Generator Copy Mechanism to achieve 100% preservation of English loanwords.
Keywords: Telugu NLP, Tenglish, Machine Translation, Neural Transliteration, Code-Mixing, Pointer-Generator Networks, IndicTrans2, Dakshina, Aksharantar.

1. Foundational Pedagogical Primer: Understanding Language, Script, and Computation

In computational linguistics, confusing language (a cognitive, semantic, and grammatical communication system) with script (a graphic orthographic system used to transcribe spoken utterances) produces severe architectural flaws. Telugu is a South-Central Dravidian language characterized by an agglutinative morphology and a canonical Subject-Object-Verb (SOV) sentence structure. In contrast, English is an analytic Germanic language with a rigid Subject-Verb-Object (SVO) typology.

Telugu is written in an Abugida (alphasyllabary) script comprising 56 distinct glyphs. Each consonant glyph possesses an inherent short vowel (/a/). When a vowel follows, the consonant is marked with an obligatory vowel diacritic (Mātrā or గుణింతం). Consonant clusters are formed using a Virama (హలంత్) to suppress the inherent vowel and conjoin a subscripted ligature (వత్తు). When users type on a standard Latin QWERTY keyboard, they do not produce Telugu Unicode code points (U+0C00 to U+0C7F); rather, they construct an ad-hoc phonetic Romanization known as Tenglish.

Core Linguistic Distinctions with Concrete Examples
  • Translation: Semantic transfer across languages: "నేను కాలేజీకి వెళ్తున్నాను" → "I am going to college."
  • Transliteration: Phonetic script mapping without semantic change: "కాలేజీ" ↔ "college / kaaleeji".
  • Code-Switching: Alternation at complete clause boundaries: "I will call you later. నాకు ఇప్పుడు అర్జెంట్ పని ఉంది."
  • Code-Mixing: Intra-sentential morpheme embedding: "naku ivala clg lo important exam undi bro."
  • Text Normalization: Standardizing noisy text: "clg ki velthunna bro" → "కాలేజీకి వెళ్తున్నాను, bro".

2. Historical Background and Technological Evolution

The progression of Telugu computational linguistics spans six distinct technological epochs:

  1. Epoch 1 (1985–1995): Rule-Based MT (RBMT) & TDIL: Handcrafted morphological analyzers and Context-Free Grammars. Stalled due to combinatorial rule explosion ($\mathcal{O}(2^n)$) and inability to handle colloquial variations.
  2. Epoch 2 (1995–2005): Paninian Grammar & Anusaaraka: Developed by Akshar Bharati (IIIT Hyderabad: Sangal, Chaitanya, Kulkarni). Formulated computational parsers based on Paninian Kāraka dependency relations mapped directly to Telugu case markers (తో, లో, కొరకు).
  3. Epoch 3 (2005–2014): Statistical MT (SMT): Phrase-based models using the Moses toolkit and GIZA++. Stalled because Telugu's agglutinative morphology generated over 200 inflected variants per verb root, causing catastrophic Out-Of-Vocabulary (OOV) data sparsity.
  4. Epoch 4 (2014–2017): Neural MT & Attention: Sequence-to-sequence recurrent networks (LSTM/GRU) with Bahdanau additive and Luong multiplicative attention. Enabled the commercial deployment of Google Neural MT (2017) and Microsoft Translator for Telugu.
  5. Epoch 5 (2017–2022): Dense Multilingual Transformers: The Transformer architecture (Vaswani et al., 2017) unlocked massive parallel scaling. Key Indian breakthroughs: Google Dakshina (Roark et al., 2020), AI4Bharat Samanantar (10.5M parallel pairs, Ramesh et al., 2022), and Aksharantar/IndicXlit (Madhani et al., 2023).
  6. Epoch 6 (2023–2026): Modern LLMs, Sarvam AI & Multimodal Speech: AI4Bharat IndicTrans2 (Gala et al., 2023), Sarvam-1 (2B Indic foundation model), and Meta SeamlessM4T v2 (multimodal speech-to-speech translation).

4. The Core Research Bottleneck: Why Canonical MT Fails on Tenglish

Standard machine translation between formal Telugu script and English is mature. The remaining research gap is driven by four linguistic pathologies:

  • Inconsistent Roman Orthography: Telugu has 56 letters, Latin has 26. A word like చాలా is typed as chala, chaala, tsala, sala.
  • Embedded English Loanword Morphology: English nouns take Telugu case suffixes (college-ki [to college], interview-lo [in interview], submit chesava? [did you submit?]).
  • Subword Tokenizer Shattering: Standard Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE) shatters "velthunnanu" into [vel, ##th, ##un, ##nan, ##u], destroying attention representations and depleting context budgets.
  • Blind Transliteration Corruption: Traditional transliteration engines blindly convert English words to Telugu script (college → కోల్లెగె).

5. Positioning of the Student's Implementation (NormMix AI)

NormMix AI is positioned as an experimental academic case-study prototype, not an industrial replacement for Google Translate. It deploys a 2-Layer Bidirectional GRU with Scaled Dot-Product Attention and a Pointer-Generator Copy Mechanism on an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4060 GPU. 

// Pointer-Generator Copy Gate Formulation
p_gen = sigmoid(w_c^T * c_t + w_s^T * s_t + w_x^T * e(y_{t-1}) + b_ptr)
P(w) = p_gen * P_vocab(w) + (1 - p_gen) * sum_{i: x_i = w} alpha_{t,i}

When generating Telugu script, $p_{\text{gen}} \approx 1.0$. When processing English loanwords (college, interview, wifi), $p_{\text{gen}} \approx 0.0$, instructing the decoder to copy the source Latin characters directly with 100% preservation fidelity.

6. Central Academic Conclusion

The major challenge in Dravidian NLP is no longer simply translating formal Telugu and English. That is a solved engineering discipline. The emerging grand challenge is understanding, normalizing, and transforming the informal, Romanized, code-mixed language actually spoken and written by 95M+ people in daily digital communication.
